## THE LEAD

Natera reported revenue of $794.0 million for the third quarter on Thursday and raised its outlook for the full year. The company's release is datelined Austin, Texas, where it operates a CLIA-certified laboratory alongside labs in San Carlos, California, and Boulder, Colorado. Revenue rose 34.1% from $592.2 million in the third quarter of 2025. The genetic testing company said the increase was driven by "an increase in volume and average selling price improvements." Natera processed about 1,093,100 tests, up 22.3%. About 339,500 were oncology tests, up 60.9%. Gross margin was 64.7%, compared with 64.9% a year earlier. The company posted a net loss of $59.9 million, or $(0.42) per diluted share. Natera raised its 2026 revenue guidance by $140 million at the midpoint, to a range of $3.005 billion to $3.035 billion from $2.850 billion to $2.910 billion. Natera lifted that outlook while posting a $59.9 million quarterly loss, so growth is still running ahead of profit.

Dallas-based Energy Transfer reported third-quarter net income attributable to partners of $1.21 billion, up from $1.02 billion a year earlier. Adjusted EBITDA rose 31% to $5.03 billion from $3.84 billion, and distributable cash flow attributable to partners, as adjusted, rose 33% to $2.52 billion. The company raised its 2026 adjusted EBITDA guidance to $19.7 billion to $19.9 billion from $18.8 billion to $19.1 billion. Growth capital is $5.4 billion to $5.6 billion for 2026 and approximately $7 billion for 2027. The distribution declared in October, $0.3425 per common unit, is a bit over 3% above a year earlier and marks the twentieth consecutive quarterly increase. The company said Phase I of the Hugh Brinson Pipeline "was placed into full service, ahead of schedule."

## UNSUITABLE FOR GENERAL RELEASE

~ Not cleared for the weekday edition.

I read every filing, release and correction this week, and I now believe institutions write each document for two readers at once, one who stops at the quote and one who reads to the bottom, on the safe bet that the second reader does not exist. This week the second reader was me. Commerce's chief executive described the quarter as "disciplined execution," and the one person who is both its CFO and its COO, as noted above, leaves Nov. 13 "to pursue another opportunity."

The quote is written for the headline, and a headline forgives everything except dullness. The rest is written for the lawyers, and lawyers forgive everything except a promise. So the quote gets warmer and the fine print gets wider. Oracle priced about $20 billion for "general corporate purposes," a heading that may include capital expenditures, acquisitions and dividends. I went through it twice looking for one thing the money could not be spent on, and I suspect coming up empty was the intended experience.

The decisions now live in the half nobody reads. Two Fermi directors, Rick Perry among them, carried most of the votes cast and lost their seats anyway, because the bylaws count all outstanding shares, and they remain on the board in the meantime. Everyone counted the votes. The bylaws counted something else.

The obvious fix is more readers. The week argues otherwise. A report on the ERCOT gas queue passed through an AI agent, a checker and a person, and still spelled the grid operator "ERCO" once. Three readers, each minding an assigned part, add up to nobody reading the whole. I know the habit because it is mine. The rule I propose, then, is one reader per document with no stake in either half, asked only whether the quote and the fine print describe the same company.

I would still take the job. I know where to look. The fine print is where Oracle keeps its dividends and Fermi keeps its election results.
