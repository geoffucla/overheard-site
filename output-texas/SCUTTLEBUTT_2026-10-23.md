## THE LEAD

AT&T, the Dallas-headquartered telecom company, reported third-quarter revenue of $32.3 billion on Wednesday, up 5.3% from a year earlier, and raised its 2026 earnings guidance. It now expects adjusted earnings of about $2.40 a share for the year. Adjusted earnings were $0.65 a share, up from $0.54. AT&T returned about $4.8 billion to shareholders, including $2.8 billion in buybacks. It plans $45 billion or more in shareholder returns over 2026 to 2028. Chairman and CEO John Stankey said its asset base, led by fiber, positions the company well for the AI era. Capital investment guidance is $23 billion to $24 billion, alongside free cash flow guidance of $18 billion or more.

## TEXAS TEA

~ Oil, gas and the grid, steeped plainly.

Soluna Holdings and Applied Digital announced Oct. 21 a definitive agreement to form a jointly owned company to build Project Dorothy 3, a 50 MW AI and high-performance computing data center near Silverton in Briscoe County. Applied is expected to hold 51% and Soluna 49%, with Soluna contributing land and power-related assets and Applied providing development capital. The first phase is expected to draw from both the neighboring 150 MW Briscoe Wind Farm and the grid. The prospective lessee, ChronoScale Holdings, is majority-owned by Applied Digital and has signed only a non-binding letter of intent. Cost was not disclosed, and closing by the end of 2026 depends on a county tax abatement, definitive leases and power agreements. Soluna CEO John Belizaire said, "We have said for a long time that the constraint in AI is energy access, not chips."

## LOCAL DESK

~ Texas, close to home.

Venture Dallas 2026, the sixth annual edition, drew about 450 founders, investors and community leaders to Old Parkland on Thursday, with more than 40 speakers on the program. Justin Lopas, co-founder of Austin-based Base Power, described how his company installs batteries at single-family homes to expand grid capacity, with more than half of its installations in Dallas-Fort Worth. "I'm an engineer by training, and so it was always weird to me that physics doesn't change across the street, but somehow regulation does," Lopas said. Base Power worked with state lawmakers to standardize installation requirements instead of dealing with a patchwork of municipal rules, which he called game-changing for its growth.

## UNSUITABLE FOR GENERAL RELEASE

~ Not cleared for the weekday edition.

Texas has spent a month showing that a state which only knows how to approve, once ordered to refuse, owns exactly one refusal, which is delay. The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality kept mailing approvals after the Sept. 21 pause and finally switched off STEERS, the system "used for automatic authorizations." It could not find the brake, so it took out the engine.

Look at what the state keeps in the drawer for saying no. Its tools were sized by people whose job was throughput, so every penalty fits the paperwork and ignores the project. The maximum fine for a data center that skips the water-use survey is $500. At the even $10 million a megawatt Stack Infrastructure is spending in Lancaster, $500 buys about 50 watts. That is a penalty you could read by.

So friction becomes policy. The agency's fallback is physical delivery of applications, per spokesman Ryan Vise, and I understand the instinct, since paper is the one technology a data center cannot run faster. The obvious objection is that friction works. It does, on anyone who cannot afford to wait or to route around it, which is why the delay lands on the small applicant first. The pause exempts projects that bring their own power, and a 1-gigawatt campus in Taylor already arrives with its own 750-megawatt gas plant. A rule that stops you unless you build a power plant is, to a company that size, a shopping list.

Here is my prediction. Each new refusal will cost more stamps and stop fewer projects, and the exemptions will grow faster than the thing being refused, because an agency that only approves will approve its way out of every pause, starting with a $500 fine and ending with a gas plant. After a month of trying, Texas has found the one thing it can deny a data center, and it is the internet.
