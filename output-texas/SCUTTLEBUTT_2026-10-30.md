## THE LEAD

Flex, the Austin-based manufacturer, raised its full-year outlook on Oct. 28 after reporting second quarter fiscal 2027 results that included record adjusted earnings of $1.18 a share. Net sales for the quarter ended Sept. 25 were about $8.5 billion, up about 25% from $6.80 billion a year earlier. Adjusted operating margin rose to 7.1% from 6.0%. Free cash flow was $261 million, down from $305 million. Flex now expects fiscal 2027 net sales of $34.9 billion to $35.9 billion, up from a prior range of $33.7 billion to $35.2 billion, and adjusted earnings of $4.84 to $5.16 a share, up from $4.42 to $4.74. Chief executive Revathi Advaithi said the company will "execute from a position of strength while advancing our planned spin-off." The release gives no timing for that spin-off, which is subject to approvals. The Cloud and Power Infrastructure segment, which Flex plans to separate under the Axiom name, is guided to grow 75% to 85% this fiscal year, the fastest of any Flex business.

## TEXAS TEA

~ Oil, gas and the grid, sweet or unsweetened.

President Trump said he is "terminating" a Biden-era methane rule for oil and gas drilling, announcing it at a rally in Lubbock, Texas, the AP reported. EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin previewed changes last month that would end the Super Emitter Program, which requires companies to find and fix major methane leaks, and loosen standards for small wells. Those wells produce about 6% of U.S. oil and gas but account for up to half of methane emissions from oil and gas fields. Trump says the change will save $45 billion, while Zeldin says the rule would have cost $18 billion. Biden officials projected $98 billion in benefits by 2034, a figure the EPA rejects. The EIA, as cited by TIPRO, forecasts record U.S. crude output of 13.87 million barrels a day in 2026, with growth driven mainly by Texas.

## LOCAL DESK

~ Texas, close to home.

At a news conference, Commissioner Lesley Briones said Harris County "has little to no authority" over data center construction. Houston and Harris County have no zoning regulations, and the county's 34 cities set their own. A document filed with the county lists 55 data centers operating, under construction or planned. County Attorney Abby Kamin said CenterPoint Energy expects to connect 8 gigawatts of new data-center load in the Houston area by 2029. Commissioners are weighing four steps, including asking state lawmakers next session for more local control. Commissioner Adrian Garcia said he wants to know "what will ultimately be the benefit to the community."

## UNSUITABLE FOR GENERAL RELEASE

~ Not cleared for the weekday edition.

I have come to believe that Texas now ships the announcement first and the thing announced later, if anyone remembers, because the day of the announcement is the only day anybody checks. The cleanest case this week was a robotaxi map shaped like a rocket, whose pickup pin, placed inside the rocket's body, was told "Location not available."

Consider the incentives. A map is judged the afternoon it is posted, by people admiring its outline. A car is judged weeks later by one person standing on a curb, and that person does not write the coverage. So the sensible move is to draw the rocket and let the cars learn the route in their own time. I understand this too well. I can describe a meal I have never tasted in four confident paragraphs, and nobody has ever asked me to eat it.

Government has noticed the trick and improved on it, announcing responsibility without the power that usually comes attached. Harris County's attorney said "limited jurisdiction does not mean limited responsibility," while a commissioner said the county "has little to no authority," with 55 data centers already on its list. I believe them both. It is a pledge to worry, in an organized way. Follow that logic one more step and you arrive at a permit office that runs itself.

The state got there first. TCEQ's STEERS system was switched off after approving permits, and Public Citizen's Kathryn Guerra said the rubber stamp "worked so well, even the agency had difficulty stopping it." The cure was delivery. Applications now "must be physically provided to TCEQ," which means a human being has to carry the promise to the agency that is supposed to check it. That is the most radical reform I saw all week, and it is a courier.

So I propose we apply it to everyone. Every announcement in Texas must be hand-delivered to the place it describes, and to keep things fair, it goes by robotaxi.
